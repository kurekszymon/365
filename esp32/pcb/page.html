Fade Lamp Board

The whole lamp controller on one 7×9 cm perfboard. A 24 V adapter feeds the LED strip through a MOSFET and the ESP32 through a buck module. A knob sets 0–60 s and a button starts the fade from the current brightness to off.

What changes from your breadboard

GPIO17 → GPIO13

The gate signal moves to IO13. On the board's top header it sits right next to GND, so the gate trace runs straight to the MOSFET without crossing anything. In led.cpp that is one line: LED_PIN = 13.

Pot on GPIO36

Use an ADC1 pin. ADC2 pins stop reading once WiFi is on. IO36 has no internal pull resistors, which suits a pot.

Button on GPIO32

The button connects the pin to GND. The internal pull-up holds it high, and C3 smooths the contact bounce, so R3 isn't needed.

JP1 jumper on 5 V

Pull JP1 before you plug in USB, so the PC and the buck don't both drive the 5 V rail. This puts your "unplug the step-down" habit from 19 Nov on the board.

230 V never touches this board.

Only the 24 V side of a closed adapter connects to J1. If your 230→24 V supply is an open frame (screw terminals for L and N), it goes in its own covered part of the case. Add an inline fuse on the 24 V lead rated about 1.25× the strip current.

The MOSFET switches the strip's negative side (low-side switching). R2 holds the gate low while the ESP32 boots, so the strip stays dark at power-up. R1 slows the gate edges a little, which reduces ringing.

Build and first power-up

  1. Check the ESP32 fits first.Plug both female headers onto the devkit, set it on the board, and check the pins land in rows M and W, columns 10–28. If your devkit's rows are 9 holes apart, use row V for the bottom header. Nothing connects to that row, so the rest of the plan stays the same.
  2. Solder the low parts first.Solder the female headers, then JP1, J3, J4, R1, R2, C3 and C4. Tack one pin of each part, check it sits straight, then solder the rest.
  3. Then the power parts.Next fit Q1 (S in F9, D in G9, G in H9), then C1, then J1 and J2. Check C1's polarity twice.
  4. Lay the high-current runs.Use 1 mm bare wire for B2→B15, B9→F9, D2→G2 and the drain run G9→G4→I4→I2. Do the signal runs after those.
  5. Mount the buck module.Stick the module on foam tape over the dashed area. Solder its four wires to F4, C12, K12 and K16.
  6. Check for shorts with the ESP32 out.Beep J1 + to −: there must be no short. Beep B2 to M15 and B2 to F9: both must beep. Beep M10 to M15: no short.
  7. Set the buck to 5 V.Leave the ESP32 out and JP1 off, then connect 24 V. Turn the trimmer until K12 reads 5.0 V against K16.
  8. Power the ESP32.Fit JP1 and check for 5 V between M10 and M15. Power off, plug in the ESP32 and power on again. The strip should stay dark until the firmware drives it.
  9. Flash over USB with JP1 off.Then fit JP1 again and connect the LED strip last.

Firmware to match

Only the pin numbers change in your current led.cpp. The knob reading below gives 0–60 s with a light snap to 0, 15, 30, 45 and 60, so the marks are easy to hit and values in between still work. Pressing the button starts the existing timer with that duration. 0 s switches off at once.

const int LED_PIN = 13;   // was 17
const int BTN_PIN = 32;   // to GND, pinMode(BTN_PIN, INPUT_PULLUP)
const int POT_PIN = 36;   // ADC1, keeps working with WiFi on

int readFadeSeconds() {
  long sum = 0;
  for (int i = 0; i < 16; i++) sum += analogRead(POT_PIN);
  float secs = (sum / 16) * 60.0f / 4095.0f;
  int mark = (int)((secs + 7.5f) / 15) * 15;          // nearest of 0/15/30/45/60
  return fabsf(secs - mark) < 2.0f ? mark : (int)(secs + 0.5f);
}

Before you order the PCB

  • Once the perfboard works, this becomes a 2-layer KiCad board of about 76×61 mm. Two layers remove the routing limits, so the GPIO choices can stay or change.
  • Make the +24 V, LED − and GND traces at least 2 mm wide (1 oz copper, up to about 5 A). Signal traces can be 0.3 mm.
  • Confirm these from the real parts first: the buck module's pad positions, the MOSFET part number, the pot type (panel or PCB mount), the button and the devkit's row spacing.
  • Add M3 mounting holes where the 3D-printed case needs them, and agree on the board outline with whoever prints the case.
  • Estimate the strip current as watts ÷ 24 V. For example, 5 m of 10 W/m COB is 50 W, about 2.1 A. A logic-level TO-220 handles that without a heatsink. After 10 minutes at 100 %, check it by touch.
